*Photogrammetry* and *3D printing* are an *art form*, an art of knowing the best methods to capture images for photogrammetry and knowing the right temperature, print speed and modeling decisions for the parts to be printed.

Most of the "art" in 3D printing is attributed to the 3D printer itself, as different printer models require different levels of "involvement" during the printing process.

Let me set the scene for what I have and what objectives I am fulfilling.

![Creality Ender V3 Pro](Printer.png)

I have access to this printer through the incubation center in my college.

# The project

I have been assigned the task of 3D printing a hand, 3D scanned by me.
The first step is obviously 3D scanning the hand.

**The 3D scanned hand after processing**
![The 3D scanned hand after processing](FinalHand.png)



The method of 3D scanning I used to achieve the result is using **Photogrammetry**. It is the practice of creating 3D models by *capturing the pictures of the subject in an ordered, orbital fashion* i.e. capturing images by circling around the subject in small steps and taking multiple pictures of said subject.

**Example from Wikipedia**
![Photogrammetry example from Wikipedia](ExampleWiki.png)



As we can see in the above image, the following was generated using photos taken by circling around the building. Similarly, we can generate 3D models for any subject.

To process the images, you need dedicated software.

# The Software
**Meshroom**
![Meshroom](Meshroom.png)

One free application is **Meshroom**, it is free and open-source and can be used to process images for 3D model creation. It is however resource intensive and expects you to have a decently powerful PC.

If you have an internet connection and some programming knowledge, you can definitely use this [*notebook*](#) to make these models, most of the models of my subjects are made using this Colab notebook.

However, for this hand, I used a different application.

**Polycam**
![Polycam](Polycam.png)



**Polycam** is available on the Apple App Store and is a suite of photogrammetry solutions, wrapped in a single app, that can be used to make 3D models and even Gaussian splats.

It was an easy process, just orbit around the subject and capture pictures.

or so it seemed...

# Model Cleaning
**Raw models (left), Final model (right, highlighted)**
![Raw models (left), final model (right, highlighted)](RawModels.png)

We took 2 scans of the entire arm, one gave a good raw of the arm, the other gave a good raw of the hand.

The scan was expected to be done with the intent to have both the hand and the arm in a single model.

However, in one scan, the subject closed his hand due to fatigue, in the other one, the application only returned the hand.

**Converting raw to final**
![Converting raw to final](RawToFinal.png)



The solution was to **remove the unwanted areas**, attach the hand onto the arm and then process the model by smoothening and fixing the details through sculpting in Blender.

But first, the models needed their different parts connected together into a single closed polygon.

## Meshlab
**Meshlab**
![Meshlab](MeshlabImport.png)


We **import** the scanned models in **Meshlab** for processing. Our first intent is to **join the different edges into one**. What do I mean by joining the different edges? To answer that, first go here:

**On the right pane**

![On the right pane](MeshlabRightPane.png)



And turn Boundary edges on:

**Boundary Edges On**

![Boundary Edges On](BoundaryEdgesToggle.png)



You will see this:

![Boundary edges](BoundaryEdges.png)

We can see **lines** highlighted across the model. These lines are enclosed and are actually the boundary of different polygons *aligned* together to form the hand.

The reason I say aligned is because their coordinates and rotations place them into the positions they are in to represent a hand, however if I sculpt on it in Blender, it will not work as intended, so we need to join them together.

To do so:

1. Select **Merge Close Vertices**

![We will select Merge Close Vertices](MergeCloseVerticesMenu.png)


2. Click **Apply**

![Merge Close Vertices dialog](MergeCloseVerticesDialog.png)


**Voila**

![The merged result](MergedResult.png)



As we can see, the moment we click apply, the model will show **no boundary edges**. This is the intended output.

Now we will export it as .obj:

![Export Mesh As](MeshlabFileMenu.png)

![Choosing the .obj file type](MeshlabExportObj.png)

The same was done by me on the other mesh.

# Challenges

1. **Print Size**

![Printer size (left), print size (right)](PrinterSize.png)

*Printer size (left), Print Size (right)*

The main problem is that our hand model is 420 mm in the Z axis (height). We need to **cut** the model into 2 parts in order to print the complete model in the same space.

2. **Print time and material used**

We need to make sure that the material used is not more than what is needed and the print time is manageable.

## Tackling the challenges

First let's import the model.

![Imported model](BambooStudio.png)

*Imported model*

# Preparing the printer

To prepare the printer we first need to **calibrate** it, i.e. dial in the print speed, temperature and other details.

Most people use the calibration cube:

![Calibration cube](CalibrationCube.png)

*Calibration Cube*

I like to use:

![Print in Place Ragdollbot on Printables](Ragdollbot.png)

*Ragdollbot*

Because it takes just an hour and yeah... it's kinda cool.

Now during the calibration test, I fortunately faced only 2 problems with simple fixes:

1. **Filament got stuck in the nozzle**

![Improper flow](ImproperFlow1.png)

*Improper flow*

![Improper flow](ImproperFlow2.png)

*Improper flow*

The **flow** of the filament became **inconsistent**, it was because the tray was too high and it resulted in the nozzle rubbing into the print and making the filament clog the nozzle partially or completely.

The fix was easy:

![Tray height adjuster](TrayHeight.png)

*Tray height adjuster*

I had to adjust the tray height, purge a small amount of the filament and continue.

2. **Filament jamming**

![Later layers didn't print](FilamentJamming.png)

*Later layers didn't print*

![Later layers didn't print, close-up](LaterLayers.png)

*Later layers didn't print*

This was because of the printer filament getting stuck in the spool:

![Filament crossing](FilamentCrossing.png)

*Filament crossing*

To fix this I corrected the crossing over of the filament in the spool.

This is a **temporary** fix. The filament pulling mechanism is strong enough to fix the spool itself, however the spool is placed at the center of the top bar:

![Spool placement](SpoolPlacement.png)

*Spool placement*

This gives the pulling mechanism a sharp angle resulting in lowered pulling force and more friction on the filament itself:

![Jamming filament](JammingFilament.png)

*Jamming filament*

A more **permanent** solution is given by this [Reddit user](#), but for it I will need some spare parts:

![Reddit solution](RedditSolution.png)

*Reddit*

But for now, manual correction does the job.
